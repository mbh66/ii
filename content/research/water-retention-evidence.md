This brief gathers the published evidence on ways of holding water and soil on land like Isikova & Ironwood, and says how far each can support a proposal for the farm. It rests on two literature searches the prospective stewards made in October 2026 for the Valley of Grace BioHub. The first found no study in the Cape Floristic Region that tests water-holding earthworks as part of restoring fynbos or renosterveld [MS]. The second asked whether restoring the soil adds water or flood protection to the gains from clearing invasive plants, and found no study of that combination either [MS]. The nearest evidence comes from Cape studies of invasive plant clearing and of renosterveld hydrology, with a few studies from other Mediterranean climates. The brief sets that evidence beside what is known of the farm's own vegetation.

## How the tags apply to research

A tag after a study's finding says whether the finding was checked in the study's own published text or abstract. [IC] means it was, and says nothing about whether other studies have repeated it. [TBV] marks a figure or detail the search reported that could not be traced to its source. Where an organisation reports on its own work, the claim carries [TBV] and the sentence names the organisation. The search and what it found are the prospective stewards' own work, tagged [MS].

## What the evidence can carry

The table gives the prospective stewards' reading of the evidence set out in the sections below [MS].

| Kind of proposal | Evidence from the Cape | What a proposal can claim | What a proposal cannot yet claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clearing invasive plants on the farm and along the river | Strong: decades of catchment studies, almost all modelled | That invasive trees use more water than the vegetation they replace, that clearing along rivers gives larger gains than clearing slopes, and that clearing is the work the Cape Water bond pays for | How much water clearing on this farm would add, until someone measures it |
| Keeping and protecting natural vegetation | Some: renosterveld studies in the Bot River and Berg River catchments, and fire studies in fynbos catchments | That natural vegetation holds soil and cleans farm runoff better than cultivated land does | That the farm, mapped mostly as fynbos, behaves as the renosterveld studies found |
| Restoring soil with compost, biochar or other amendments | None in the Cape; nutrient studies after clearing point to risk | That the question is open, and that adding nutrients to these soils risks favouring invasive plants | Any benefit for water, soil or plants |
| Reducing erosion and flood damage after fire | Some: a Cape Peninsula study of erosion after fire | That invaded land burns hotter and loses far more soil than fynbos | That any measure on the farm would lower flood peaks downstream |
| Stabilising eroded banks and gullies | Thin: one practitioner report on renosterveld watercourses | That a Cape conservation body has used these measures and judges them worthwhile | Any measured gain in water or fall in sediment |
| A measured trial of water-holding earthworks | None in fynbos or renosterveld | That the question is open, and that a measured result from the farm would be new evidence | That earthworks will add water, or that a gain could be sold to a water fund |

## The vegetation on the farm

The farm is portion 10 of the farm Broken Hill No. 88, and the Surveyor General's cadastre gives it 199.66 hectares [IC]. The South African National Biodiversity Institute's national vegetation map, published through the Western Cape Government's spatial data warehouse, shows which vegetation type is expected on each part of the country [IC].

The prospective stewards checked 13 points spread across the inside of the farm's Surveyor General boundary against the map [MS]. Twelve fall in South Langeberg Sandstone Fynbos, and one, near the north-western boundary, falls in Swellendam Silcrete Fynbos [MS]. The 2018 National Biodiversity Assessment lists South Langeberg Sandstone Fynbos as least concern and Swellendam Silcrete Fynbos as endangered [IC]. The nearest renosterveld on the map is Eastern Rûens Shale Renosterveld, less than two kilometres south of the farm [MS], which the same assessment lists as endangered [IC].

A fire burned the farm in August 2025 [MS]. The farm's manager, who oversaw the fire, described it to the prospective stewards, and they have seen the burned land themselves [MS].

The national map is drawn for the whole country, so what grows on a given hectare is settled by a survey on the ground. A botanical assessment of the farm dated 8 November 2021 exists, and the 2024 planning decision found part of the planned building footprint in an area of medium-high botanical sensitivity [IC]. This brief has not seen the assessment. [[documents/municipal-approval-2024|Municipal Approval for Additional Homes (2024)]] summarises the decision.

If the map is right, the renosterveld studies below describe the land south of the farm more closely than the farm itself [TBV]. The fynbos catchment studies on clearing and fire apply more directly [TBV].

## Clearing invasive plants

Van Wilgen and Wannenburgh (2016) review South Africa's Working for Water programme, which clears invasive alien plants to conserve water and create employment [IC]. The programme began in the mid-1990s because invasive trees use more water than the vegetation they replace [IC]. The search attributed figures for hectares cleared and water gained to the review, and none could be traced to it, so this brief quotes none of them [MS]. Clearing works by reducing the water that plants draw from the soil; it builds nothing that slows or sinks water on the land [IC].

Across South Africa, Le Maitre and colleagues (2016) estimate that invasive alien plants reduce surface flows by about 1,444 million cubic metres a year, 2.9 percent of the natural runoff [IC]. Researchers who modelled four small mountain catchments above Cape Town's dams report that clearing fully invaded catchments could raise streamflow by 15 to 30 percent, that clearing along rivers gives gains almost twice those from clearing the slopes around them, and that the gains are largest in low flows and dry years [TBV]. Those figures come from the researchers' public account, and their paper was not located. A related study found that clearing at moderate levels of invasion would have offset 3 to 16 percentage points of the streamflow lost in the Cape Town drought of 2015 to 2017 [IC].

The [[institutions/greater-cape-town-water-fund|Greater Cape Town Water Fund]] applies the same practice to Cape Town's supply catchments. Its 2018 business case found that clearing supplies water at about a tenth of the cost per kilolitre of the alternatives it compared [IC]. It warned that cleared land must be kept clear for the gains to last, and that burned land needs prompt follow-up so that regrowth does not come back denser [IC]. The Fund's work lies outside the Duiwenhoks catchment [MS].

Clearing of the same kind is under way above the farm. By May 2025 [[institutions/helihack-initiative|HeliHack]] operations had removed 26,913 pines from 3,880 hectares of the [[the-land/boosmansbos-wilderness-area|Boosmansbos Wilderness Area]] [IC]. No study found for this site measures how much water that clearing adds to the Duiwenhoks Dam [MS]. A gauging station near the farm recorded the river only from 1972 to 1984 [IC]. [[the-land/cape-water-bond|The Cape Water Bond and Clearing Around the Farm]] gives the detail.

The 2024 planning decision already requires a plan to control and remove invasive alien plants on the farm [IC].

## Keeping natural vegetation

De Waal (2023), a Stellenbosch University doctoral thesis, studied the renosterveld landscape of the Bot River catchment in the Overberg with a hydrological model and a year of river monitoring [IC]. It modelled soil erosion in the catchment at 22 tonnes per square kilometre a year under natural vegetation and 490 tonnes under the land use of 2018 [IC]. The thesis links nutrient loading in the river to farming, with nitrate and nitrite nitrogen periodically above the guidelines for aquatic ecosystems [IC]. It found that renosterveld fragments clean polluted runoff from farmland [IC]. The thesis monitors and models; it tests no restoration measure [IC].

Vermeulen (2010), a Stellenbosch University master's thesis, compared a mature renosterveld stand with a nearby wheat field in the Berg River catchment and reported large differences in groundwater levels and salinity between the two [IC].

Studies of fire in fynbos catchments point the same way. A 1994 University of Natal thesis reports that two fynbos catchments showed no change in storm flow after fire, while two plantation catchments showed large increases in storm flow and sediment yield [IC].

From Spain, Andreu and colleagues (1998) measured 56 erosive rain events on experimental plots between 1988 and 1995 [IC]. Natural shrub vegetation cut sediment production by almost 75 percent compared with bare soil, and the best of three planted shrubs by more than 58 percent [IC]. The study tests planting with no earthworks. Shrub cover plays the same protective role in fynbos and renosterveld [TBV].

The 2024 planning decision links the farm's environmental management plan to a stewardship agreement with CapeNature [IC]. [[institutions/capenature|CapeNature]] describes the stewardship programme.

## Fire and fynbos soils

Fisher (2025), a Stellenbosch University doctoral thesis, found that soil repels water in five of six native fynbos vegetation types and under invasive alien stands [IC]. The effect was strongest in the hot, dry summer months, and the top 20 millimetres of soil repelled water less than the soil below [IC]. A planned burn reduced the effect at the surface of a Protea-dominated site and not at a Restio-dominated site, and the changes after fire were short-lived [IC]. In the drought of April 2015, soil that usually took up water became repellent, and seedlings and adult plants died [IC].

Euston-Brown (2000) studied catchments on the Cape Peninsula after fire [IC]. Fires burned much hotter in invaded and cleared land than in fynbos [IC]. Erosion at fynbos sites never exceeded 1.5 centimetres, and on average the soil surface rose [IC]. After the first winter rains, invaded land lost a little over 100 cubic metres of soil per hectare [IC].

The farm burned in August 2025 [MS], so its soils are in the period these studies describe. How the fire behaved on the farm, and what it left, has not been recorded on this site [MS].

## Restoring soil

The second search found no study in fynbos or renosterveld of compost, biochar, microbial inoculants or planned grazing as ways to restore soil [MS]. The studies it found come from other Mediterranean regions and have not been checked [TBV].

The Cape evidence on nutrients points the other way. Fynbos soils are poor in nutrients, and their plants are adapted to that. Yelenik, Stock and Richardson (2004) found more available nitrogen in soils under invasive *Acacia saligna* than under fynbos, and found that a native grass grew faster on the richer soils, which could let it crowd out slower fynbos seedlings after clearing [IC]. They recommend burning and mulch rich in carbon to draw the nitrogen down [IC]. Ruwanza, Musil and Esler (2012) added sucrose to lowland fynbos soils to lock up nitrogen; it slowed plant growth without lowering soil nitrogen, and they judged it ineffective as a restoration aid [IC].

## Stabilising eroded banks and gullies

The Overberg Renosterveld Conservation Trust reports a three-year project to restore renosterveld watercourses, funded by the WWF Nedbank Green Trust from September 2015 [TBV]. The Trust reports mulching livestock paths to stabilise them and using portable electric fences to keep livestock off recovering ground [TBV]. It says that leaving watercourses to recover on their own is not enough, and that restoring them benefits renosterveld [TBV]. The search found no measurements of infiltration, runoff or soil moisture in the report [MS].

Olivier, Van De Wiel and De Clercq (2023) review gully erosion across South Africa. They report that human activity is a major present-day driver of gullying, and that gullying is most pronounced in the centre and east of the country [IC]. They call for research on mitigation measures and how well they work [IC].

The Kromme River in the Eastern Cape is the nearest record of structures meeting a large flood. Thirteen structures were built there from 2000 to protect palmiet wetlands [IC]. The sources disagree on how they fared in the flood of August 2006. SANBI, which runs Working for Wetlands, reports that the structures did their job and the main wetland basins came through largely intact [TBV]. A Rhodes University inspection for the Water Research Commission found every structure damaged, four of them badly [IC].

The second search reported erosion along the Duiwenhoks itself: a donga in the upper Duiwenhoks wetland widening since 1972, ongoing flood erosion at a causeway on the river at Heidelberg, and a 2013 flood that ate away a riverbank north of Heidelberg until a gravel road collapsed [TBV]. None of these could be traced to a source for this page.

Along the Duiwenhoks, the [[institutions/grootvadersbosch-conservancy|Grootvadersbosch Conservancy]] reports stretches where the floodplain is severely eroded, and recommends stabilising the banks [TBV]. Whether the banks and slopes on the farm are eroded is not known [MS]. [[history/western-cape-floods|Floods in the Western Cape, 2003 to 2026]] records the floods that have washed soil from farmland in the region.

## Earthworks that hold water

The [Water Retention Landscapes](https://wiki.bioconomy.earth/concepts/water-retention-landscapes) page on the BioConomy wiki describes land shaped and planted so that rain soaks in and reaches streams slowly. Earthworks used for this include swales, keyline patterns, contour bunds, leaky weirs and gully plugs. The search paired terms for these practices with fynbos and renosterveld, in English and in three Afrikaans search strings, and found no study that applies or evaluates any of them in restoring either vegetation [MS].

The nearest examples come from other Mediterranean climates.

- **Tamera, southern Portugal.** Tamera reports building a water retention landscape since 2006, with keyline design, terraces, swales and 29 lakes and retention spaces by 2015 [TBV]. Its claims of water self-sufficiency and stabilised groundwater come from the project's own documentation, as summarised by two European Union platforms [TBV]. The search found no peer-reviewed measurement of infiltration, runoff or recharge there [MS]. The vegetation there is oak savanna, which differs from fynbos and renosterveld [TBV].
- **Central Chile.** Toro-Mujica (2023), a conference paper, reports a trial on three farms in which keyline design influenced soil moisture and temperature and the quality of the grasses [IC]. The land is naturalised annual grassland, and the trial measured soil and forage, with no measure of water flowing through the land [IC].

How earthworks would behave on land that burns has not been studied in fynbos or renosterveld [MS].

The literature review sets out what a first study would measure [MS]. A site would be recorded before work starts and compared over time with a site left alone, recording:

- how fast water soaks into the soil;
- soil moisture at several depths;
- runoff and peak flow;
- sediment carried off the land;
- groundwater level;
- flow in the stream below;
- plant cover and species;
- cost, compared with clearing and fire management.

The review proposes at least three years of records, to catch dry and wet seasons, and one fire cycle where fire is part of management [MS]. The protected land beside the farm could serve as the site left alone. No rainfall or soil moisture record for the farm is known to this site [MS].

## Measuring the result

The Greater Cape Town Water Fund's 2019 business case found that the outcomes of Working for Water are counted in hectares treated and jobs created, with no measure in water [IC]. The Fund's own water gains were modelled [IC]. A water gain from clearing or soil work on the farm would need its own measurements to be counted, and the river near the farm has had no gauge since 1984 [IC].

The second review describes a programme of 10 to 20 years, with at least two years of records before any work starts, untreated land kept as a control, and rainfall simulation to test treatments against extreme storms [MS]. A proposal will set out how such a study could run on the farm.

## Limits of the search

The first search ran on the open web on 12 October 2026 [MS]. It did not query the Scopus or Web of Science databases, and several sources sat behind paywalls [MS]. Its Afrikaans coverage was thin [MS]. The second search reports using those databases, and its results could not be confirmed [MS]. The absence of a direct study is therefore held as a working hypothesis. A search of the databases themselves, and questions to the authors and organisations cited here, would test it.

## What is still open

- Which vegetation grows on the farm, and in what condition. The botanical assessment of 8 November 2021 belongs to the company, and this site will say nothing on it until the company has replied.
- Whether Swellendam Silcrete Fynbos or Garden Route Shale Fynbos, both mapped close by, reach inside the farm's boundary.
- Whether the farm takes in the river bank, and whether its banks, slopes or old lands show erosion.
- Whether any rainfall, stream flow or soil record exists for the farm.
- How hot the August 2025 fire burned on the farm, and how the soil and plants have come back since.
- Whether the erosion reported along the Duiwenhoks concerns the stretch near the farm.
- Whether a database search finds the study this search did not.
- How water-holding earthworks would interact with fire in fynbos.
- Whether the organisations named here agree with how this page describes their work. None has yet been asked.
