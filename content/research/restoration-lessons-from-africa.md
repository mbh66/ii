This brief asks what community-led land restoration elsewhere in Africa can tell the people of Isikova & Ironwood about caring for their land and the country around it. It rests on a review of sixteen initiatives that the prospective stewards made in October 2026, starting from the Muonde Trust in Mazvihwa, Zimbabwe [MS]. The review found that the initiatives which have lasted for decades grew from practices local people already used and from secure rights to land, trees or wildlife, and that their formal bodies came after many years of shared work [MS]. It found little independent evaluation: most outcome figures, Muonde's among them, come from the initiatives themselves [MS]. The cases differ from the farm in land tenure, rainfall, vegetation and the make-up of the community, so the brief sets out how far each finding reaches.

> **This brief records evidence. It proposes nothing.** Any idea drawn from it will have its own page in [[proposals/index|Proposals]]. The company and its Community Management Panel have not yet replied to this page. The Muonde Trust, Friends of Muonde, the Overberg Renosterveld Conservation Trust, the Gouritz Cluster Biosphere Reserve, [[institutions/capenature|CapeNature]], the [[institutions/grootvadersbosch-conservancy|Grootvadersbosch Conservancy]] and the other organisations named here have not yet been asked to comment on how it describes their work.

This brief keeps only what bears on the farm.

## The Muonde Trust

Muonde works in Mazvihwa Communal Area in Zvishavane District, south-central Zimbabwe, an area of variable rain in the lowest-potential farming zone of the country's classification [TBV]. Muonde reports rainfall below 500 millimetres a year [TBV]. It describes its community as one chiefdom made up of many clans, speaking one language [TBV].

**How it began.** Abraham Mawere Ndhlovu, from a village in Mazvihwa, joined a research team there in 1986 [TBV]. He and Ken Wilson, an outside researcher, are named as Muonde's founders and have interviewed Mazvihwa farmers since the mid-1980s [TBV]. Muonde describes the trust as an idea "formalized in 2008 and completed in 2014 among friends who have worked together in Mazvihwa since the mid-1980s" [TBV]. On that account, more than twenty years of shared work came before the trust.

**How it is run.** Muonde states that its board has five women and three men from Mazvihwa, plus one external adviser, and that its staff are mostly women, all from Mazvihwa [TBV]. It says its programmes "emerge, grow and evolve depending on what is exciting the community" [TBV]. Its stated method is to find people in the area who are already solving a problem and spread their solutions from farmer to farmer [TBV]. Muonde says chiefs bless each new initiative, and UNDP reports that the chief and his headman set up committees to protect forests in response to Muonde's work [TBV].

**Its record and its model.** Muonde's research team of about thirty people has recorded the area's crops, cattle and woodland for over three decades [TBV]. In 2020 the team and outside modellers published a computer model of one village's land built from that record [IC]. In the model, storing grain from good years to carry into bad ones did more than any other measure to keep the system going, and outside support that cut the link between herds and grazing made the system less stable [IC]. The paper, co-written by Muonde's founders, reports that Muonde used the model with local leaders to agree that farmers return to old fields before clearing woodland grazing [TBV].

**Its money.** Friends of Muonde, a project of Earth Island Institute in California, raises money from individuals and small foundations and makes grants to registered Zimbabwean non-profits, Muonde among them [TBV]. Project grants, such as one from a UNDP climate fund, come on top [TBV]. Fundraising sits with a separate body, and decisions sit in Mazvihwa.

**How it spread.** Muonde reports that 34 organisations have visited to learn its water harvesting and dry stone walling [TBV]. In December 2024 it ran a five-day training in a village of about 150 households in Monze District, Zambia; the team first sought the consent of local authorities, then trained three residents to interpret and carry the work on [TBV]. That reach came almost four decades after the work began [TBV].

**The evidence.** The review found no independent evaluation of Muonde [MS]. Its published papers are co-written by its own researchers, so its outcome claims stay [TBV] until an outside study tests them.

## What the other cases add

### Local practice came first

Farmer Managed Natural Regeneration began in Niger in 1983 with twelve farmers protecting shoots from tree stumps already in their fields, and spread from farmer to farmer across about 5 million hectares [IC]. In Shinyanga, Tanzania, a government programme from 1986 to 2004 helped Sukuma communities revive *ngitili*, their own dry-season fodder reserves, across roughly 300,000 hectares; its early planting of exotic trees failed for lack of local ownership [IC]. A study of Shinyanga concludes that "local community incorporation/ownership is key" [IC].

### Time before institutions

The Shinyanga programme ran for eighteen years [IC]. The Overberg Renosterveld Conservation Trust reports that one land purchase took sixteen years of negotiation [TBV]. In northern Kenya the reverse sequence went to court: in January 2025 a Kenyan court found that two community conservancies supported by the Northern Rangelands Trust had been set up without proper public participation [IC].

### Rights under law

Namibia's communal conservancies grew after a 1996 law gave communities conditional rights over wildlife and tourism [IC]. Shinyanga's village by-laws took force under Tanzania's 1999 village land legislation [IC]. In the Overberg, a farmer who signs a conservation easement registers it on the title deed, keeps the land, and binds later owners; the trust supports clearing, burning, fencing and wildlife work in return [TBV].

### Records

The UN's 2020 status report on the Great Green Wall across the Sahel found about 4 percent of its intervention zone restored after thirteen years, and named weak monitoring among the obstacles [IC]. A review of South Africa's Working for Water programme from 1998 to 2020 found that its effectiveness was not monitored regularly [IC].

### Money

Muonde's funding passes through a foreign foundation to a locally governed trust [TBV]. In the Eastern Cape, Commonland funds separate local companies for honeybush tea, essential oils and rehabilitation [TBV]. The Northern Rangelands Trust's carbon project was suspended by its registry after the 2025 court ruling [IC]. Studies of Working for Water find that workers came to depend on project work [IC]. The [[the-land/cape-water-bond|Cape Water bond]] pays on verified outcomes from clearing [IC].

### Grazing claims

A 2017 meta-analysis compared Holistic Planned Grazing with continuous grazing in studies from 1972 to 2016 and found no difference in plant cover, plant biomass or animal gain [IC]. The positive studies came mostly from wetter sites [IC]. The Savory Institute disputes how the studies were chosen [TBV].

## What exists near the farm

The district already holds several of the tools the cases point to.

- **Stewardship with CapeNature.** CapeNature's programme offers private landowners four options, from flexible conservation areas to contract nature reserves [IC]. The 2024 approval for additional homes requires the farm's environmental management plan to be linked to a stewardship agreement with CapeNature [IC]. [[institutions/capenature|CapeNature]] describes the programme, and this site says nothing more on the agreement until the company has replied.
- **Easements in the Overberg.** In 2021 the Overberg Renosterveld Conservation Trust reported three new easements that join renosterveld corridors between Swellendam and Heidelberg over more than 10 kilometres [TBV]. The trust works in renosterveld. The farm is mapped mostly as South Langeberg Sandstone Fynbos, with the nearest mapped renosterveld less than two kilometres to the south [MS].
- **A biosphere reserve.** The Gouritz Cluster Biosphere Reserve, designated by UNESCO on 9 June 2015, covers about 3.19 million hectares across parts of the Western and Eastern Cape [IC]. It runs river and corridor projects in Hessequa with landowners [TBV]. Whether the farm lies inside its boundary has not been checked [MS].
- **Conservancies.** The [[institutions/grootvadersbosch-conservancy|Grootvadersbosch Conservancy]] says its 19 landowners cover about 35,000 hectares west of the farm [TBV]. A conservancy also looks after the Duiwenhoks estuary [TBV].
- **Clearing above the farm.** CapeNature, the conservancy, landowners and volunteers cleared invasive pines from the dam's upper catchment in April 2023 [TBV]. [[institutions/helihack-initiative|The HeliHack Initiative]] describes that work.
- **The community's own rules.** The constitution lists removing alien plants, planting indigenous species, low-tillage methods, hedgerows and swales among its conservation priorities, bans chemical pesticides and herbicides, and lists membership of the Southern Cape Fire Protection Association among its fire-safety obligations [MS]. Its CMP portfolios include Environment and Energy-Water-Waste [MS].

## Where the cases differ from the farm

- **Land tenure.** Muonde works in a communal area [IC], where it says chiefs bless each initiative [TBV]. Shinyanga's enclosures rest on village by-laws [IC]. Isikova & Ironwood is owned by a company [MS]. Its constitution sends community matters to the CMP, which advises the Board, and its shareholders' agreement governs legal matters [MS]. A chief's blessing has no counterpart here. Decisions on title, servitudes and contracts sit with the Board [MS].
- **The community.** Mazvihwa is one chiefdom with many clans [TBV]. The farm has room for up to seven households [MS]. Communities along the N2 coast fall under more than one municipality and have their own histories [TBV].
- **Scale.** Muonde works across parts of two wards [TBV]. The farm covers 199.66 hectares on the Surveyor General's record [IC].
- **Rain, vegetation and fire.** Mazvihwa is semi-arid, and the water harvesting the cases describe was developed for short rainy seasons [TBV]. The Duiwenhoks catchment receives rain throughout the year, with peaks in autumn and spring [IC]. The farm is mapped mostly as fynbos, which burns [MS], and a fire burned the farm in August 2025 [MS].
- **Who the outsiders are.** Muonde's outside founder came as a researcher and worked alongside local people for decades [TBV]. Prospective stewards would join a company and sign its shareholders' agreement and constitution, and the CMP gives incoming shareholders an induction [MS].

## Limits of the search

- The review was one research run in October 2026, drawing on published sources found on the web; the main citations were checked against their publishers' records [MS].
- It found no independent evaluation of Muonde [MS].
- It did not cover Ethiopia's national watershed programmes, several East African initiatives, or cases in Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda and Senegal [MS].
- It found no case on company-owned land in fynbos with a resident community, the arrangement closest to this farm [MS].
- Where sources give different figures, the full review reports all of them.
