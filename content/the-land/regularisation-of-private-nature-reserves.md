Regularisation is the name CapeNature gives to checking whether the Western Cape's older private nature reserves meet the National Environmental Management: Protected Areas Act of 2003, and helping their owners comply where they do not [IC]. Many of these reserves were declared under the Cape's Nature Conservation Ordinance of 1974, which asked less of them than the Act does [IC]. The [[the-land/hessequa-hills-private-nature-reserve|Hessequa Hills Private Nature Reserve]], about 700 metres from the farm, is one of them [TBV].

[[institutions/capenature|CapeNature]] has not yet been asked to comment on this page. The page describes a process in law and in CapeNature's published statements. It says nothing about the status of any particular reserve beyond what a public register shows. The key to the letters in square brackets is on [[about|About This Site]].

## Why it is needed

From 1974 a landowner in the Cape could ask the provincial Minister responsible for nature conservation to approve a private nature reserve on their own land [IC]. The application gave the land's boundaries, proof of ownership and the proposed name [IC]. If the Minister approved it, the reserve's name, boundaries and conditions were published in the Provincial Gazette [IC]. The owner then had to manage the land to protect and preserve its plants and animals, under any conditions the Minister had set [IC].

The Protected Areas Act came into force in 2003. Section 12 of the Act treats an area already protected under provincial law as a nature reserve or protected environment, as long as the purpose it was protected for fits one of those two categories [IC]. Private nature reserves established under the Ordinance therefore count as nature reserves under the Act [IC].

The Act asks more of a nature reserve than the Ordinance did. It needs a formally appointed management authority, an approved management plan and an endorsement on the title deed [IC]. CapeNature's draft Protected Area Expansion Strategy for 2021 to 2025 estimated that only about 40% of the province's protected areas met all three [IC]. So the legal standing of each older reserve has to be checked, one at a time [IC].

## The national rules

In November 2017 the Minister of Environmental Affairs published norms and standards for including private nature reserves in the national register of protected areas [IC]. They apply to land treated as a nature reserve under section 12 of the Act, land declared under section 23(5), and private land declared a nature reserve under section 23(3) [IC]. Their stated aims are to verify that each private nature reserve exists, to recognise it as a protected area, to keep its natural integrity over the long term, and to ensure that only verified reserves enter the national register [IC].

### Verification

A reserve is verified when two things are shown [IC]:

| Requirement | What has to be shown |
|---|---|
| Proof of declaration | A copy of the gazetted notice, sent by the provincial MEC to the national Minister, showing the gazette number and date, the section of the law it was made under, the reserve's name and description, its survey diagram, and the endorsed title deed [IC] |
| Compliance with the Act | Written notice of the agreements in force with the landowners, an appointed management authority, and a management plan submitted for approval [IC] |

The management plan must include plans for managing biodiversity or particular species, a plan for removing alien and invasive species, and a zonation plan [IC].

### Standards a reserve must keep

| Standard | What it requires |
|---|---|
| Purpose | The reserve is run for the reason it was declared [IC] |
| Ecological integrity | A review from satellite or aerial images confirms that no habitat has been destroyed, no natural vegetation has been ploughed, and building matches the management and zoning plans [IC] |
| Field inspection | The provincial conservation authority checks the review on the ground, checks that the management plan is being carried out, especially the clearing of invasive species, and checks that no species foreign to the reserve has been introduced and no stud breeding is taking place [IC] |
| Subdivision | Land may be subdivided only where the management plan allows it. A subdivided piece that conflicts with the plan is taken out of the reserve [IC] |
| Control | The management authority controls access. The boundaries are clearly marked and publicly known [IC] |
| Reporting | The management authority reports to the MEC on how far the reserve meets the norms. Each provincial authority sends the Minister its list of verified reserves within a month of the end of each financial year [IC] |

The norms also set out the owner's position. The owner keeps the constitutional right to their property and its lawful use [IC]. In return the owner must avoid harming the reserve's integrity, take reasonable steps against degradation, keep development within the law and the management plan, and cooperate openly with the state [IC].

### When a reserve does not comply

A reserve that falls short of the norms needs an action plan, with timeframes, to put it right [IC]. Where a reserve is badly degraded or has changed beyond repair, the Minister or the MEC may withdraw its declaration under section 24 of the Act [IC]. The Minister may remove a private nature reserve from the national register only after its declaration has been withdrawn [IC].

## CapeNature's project

| Date | Event | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| 1974 | The Nature Conservation Ordinance provides for private nature reserves | [IC] |
| 2003 | The Protected Areas Act passed; older provincial reserves treated as nature reserves under it | [IC] |
| 3 November 2017 | National norms and standards for verifying private nature reserves published | [IC] |
| 2019 | CapeNature begins its verification project, funded by the Table Mountain Fund | [IC] |
| July 2022 | CapeNature reports 155 private nature reserves identified, 111 reviewed and more than half of those owners willing to regularise | [IC] |
| 15 November 2022 | The repeal of the Ordinance by the Western Cape Biodiversity Act comes into force | [IC] |

CapeNature describes the project in four stages [IC]:

1. Find the province's private nature reserves. The project identified 155.
2. Decide which to look at first.
3. Review the biodiversity of the reserves chosen. All 111 reviewed still had high biodiversity value.
4. Work with the owners and support those willing to regularise.

The draft expansion strategy says the work is carried out by Conservation Outcomes with CapeNature, with funding from the Table Mountain Fund [IC]. CapeNature names Conservation Outcomes and Conservation@Work as partners that keep owners engaged and share knowledge with them [IC]. The project is a pilot, and CapeNature intends the method to be used in other provinces [IC]. CapeNature reports that it met several challenges in the legal process [IC].

The draft strategy leaves private nature reserves out of the province's protected area total while they are being verified [IC]. It sets two actions for them. Reserves with high biodiversity value and willing owners are to be converted into nature reserves that comply fully with the Act [IC]. For reserves with low biodiversity value or unwilling owners, CapeNature and the national department are to investigate withdrawal [IC]. The strategy aims to raise the share of compliant protected areas from about 40% to 50% [IC].

The same strategy says CapeNature supports four municipalities, Hessequa among them, with the status of the nature reserves they own [IC].

## The Western Cape Biodiversity Act

The Western Cape Biodiversity Act of 2021 repeals the 1974 Ordinance in full [IC]. Nature reserves established under sections 6, 7 or 12 of the Ordinance continue to exist, as nature reserves or protected environments under the Protected Areas Act [IC]. The repeal and that transitional provision came into force on 15 November 2022 [IC]. Since then no new private nature reserve can be declared under the Ordinance [TBV]. New reserves on private land go through CapeNature's Biodiversity Stewardship programme, which is set out on the [[institutions/capenature|CapeNature]] page [IC].

## What regularisation brings an owner

A nature reserve declared under the Protected Areas Act is excluded from municipal property rates on the parts not used for commercial, business, farming or residential purposes [IC]. CapeNature's fact sheet says this needs only a declaration under the Act [IC]. Two income tax deductions need more: section 37D needs a declaration under the Act and a title deed endorsement of at least 99 years, and section 37C(3) needs a declaration of at least 30 years [IC]. The sources checked do not say whether a reserve declared under the Ordinance and not yet regularised qualifies for either.

## What is still open

- How many of the 155 reserves have been regularised since 2022, and how many have been withdrawn. CapeNature has published no later figures that this page found.
- Whether Hessequa Hills Private Nature Reserve has been reviewed, and whether its owners have agreed to regularise.
- Who appoints the management authority for a regularised private reserve, and whether the owner can be that authority.
- How long the steps take, and what they cost the owner.
- Whether a reserve that is not regularised keeps its legal protection until its declaration is withdrawn.
