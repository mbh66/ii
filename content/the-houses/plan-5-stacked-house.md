> **These plans are not agreed.** The Haupt family drew them in October 2026 for discussion among themselves. Nobody outside the family has been asked: not the company, its Community Management Panel (CMP) or its Board, not the residents and not Hessequa Municipality. They will be put to the company through the CMP once the family has agreed what to propose.

## The goal

> Stack the two halves on one small slab, so the house uses the least ground and the least digging.

The house is 12.5 metres by 8 metres, two storeys high. Half B takes the ground floor beside the shared core. The core holds the plant room and laundry, and the stair to Half A, with Half A's own front door at ground level. Half A takes the whole upper floor, 100 m², with two bedrooms and its living room along the north wall.

The ground floor rises 2.95 metres to the upper floor. The roof rises to the south, from an eave about 5.4 metres above the slab at the north wall to a ridge about 6.2 metres above it at the south.

## How each household would use it

- **House 1.** Veronica on the ground floor, Michael and his daughter above.
- **House 2.** The siblings' mother and her partner on the ground floor, with no stair. Robert and his wife above, one flight away if they are needed.

## On the three sites

The site figures come from [[the-houses/layout-d-site-development-plan|Layout D]], which modelled each platform from 10-metre contours. They are [TBV] until the platforms have been walked.

- **Unit 3.** The house uses 12.5 metres of contour and 100 m² of slab, the least of the six plans, which suits a site where only about a third of the Area is gentle ground [TBV]. Across its 8-metre depth the ground still falls about 1.8 metres [TBV]. If the slab is half cut and half filled, the north eave would stand about 6.3 metres above the ground below it. If the slab stands fully on fill, the north eave would be about 7.2 metres above that ground, over the limit [TBV]. On Unit 3 the slab would need a shallow cut at its south edge.
- **Unit 1.** About 0.6 metres of fall [TBV], so the north eave stands about 6 metres above the ground with the slab fully on fill. A good fit, with much of the platform left for gardens.
- **Main Dwelling.** About 1 metre of fall [TBV]. The shared building, Part B, could join the ground floor beside the core. A fair fit, with the tallest of the buildings in the cluster beside the shared facility.

## Pros

- Half the slab and half the roof of the single-storey plans, and the least ground disturbed.
- The smallest house on the land, which leaves the most of the Area open.
- Both halves keep a full north wall of windows, and Half A gains the view from the upper floor.
- Half B is fully step-free on the ground floor.

## Cons

- A double storey needs the approval of every shareholder [MS].
- The house stands close to the 6.5-metre limit, and the approval asks for homes screened from neighbours, the roads and the nature reserves within view [IC].
- The roof catches about half the rain of the single-storey plans: about 100 litres for each millimetre that falls.
- Half A has no garden at its own door, and its households carry everything up a flight of stairs.
- Sound travels down through the upper floor into Half B. The floor needs to be built to stop it.
- Half A has one stair. It needs a window or a balcony large enough to leave by if the stair is blocked by fire.

## What is still unknown

- Whether every shareholder would approve a double storey.
- How the municipality measures height on a slope, which decides whether Plan 5 fits on Unit 3.
- Whether a home of two halves counts as one dwelling. [[the-houses/house-plans|House Plans]] sets out the question.
