They will be put to the company through the CMP once the family has agreed what to propose.

## The goal

> Hold both halves and their shared kitchen inside the shape with the least wall for its floor, a circle, and test whether every main room can still take the winter sun.

## Why a circle

- A circle about 16 metres across holds 200 m². Its outside wall is about 50 metres long. Plan 1's is 66 metres, and the courtyard house and the H-plan have nearly 90 metres. Wall is one of the largest costs of a house, and the way most of its heat leaves.
- A round house sheds wind from any direction. The knoll is the most exposed ground in the Residential Zone [TBV], and its wind has not been recorded.
- Veronica's first choice is an earthship [MS], and Robert taught himself to build in relation to the land [MS]. Stone or earth walls under a reciprocal-frame roof of poles bring the two together.
- Both halves must be joined under a single roof [MS]. A round roof is one roof by nature.

The house is built as a sixteen-sided plan, close enough to a circle to work as one, with flat walls that take ordinary windows and roof sheets. A line from north to south divides it: Half A is the west half and Half B the east, each 84 m².

The shared core is 32 m² in three parts: the kitchen and hearth at the centre, 15 m², lit from above by a glazed lantern at the top of the roof; a garden room of 10 m² on the north, between the two living rooms, with doors to the patio; and the laundry and plant room of 7 m² on the south, between the two front doors.

Each half has a passage running round the hub, which keeps its rooms private from the kitchen, and five rooms on the outer wall:

- **The living room,** 20 m², facing north-west in Half A and north-east in Half B.
- **Bedroom 1,** 18 m², facing west or east.
- **A step-free bathroom,** 11 m².
- **Bedroom 2,** 18 m², facing south-west or south-east.
- **The entry,** 6 m², with the half's own front door on the south.

Every room is on one level. The rooms are wedges, about 5.6 metres wide at the outer wall and 2.4 metres at the passage, so the narrow inner ends take cupboards and shelves.

### The test of the sun

The plan answers the question it was drawn to ask. Only the north arc of the circle, about a third of its wall, takes the winter sun at noon. The two living rooms and the garden room stand on that arc. The first bedrooms face east and west and take the morning or the evening sun. The second bedrooms face south-east and south-west and take little winter sun. The kitchen at the centre takes its light from the lantern. A round plan gives the main living rooms the sun, and cannot give it to every room.

### One roof

The roof is a reciprocal frame of timber poles at about 15°, each rafter resting on the next, from eaves about 2.8 metres above the floor to a glazed lantern at the centre about 5.4 metres above it [TBV]. Rain falls all the way round, so the gutter runs the full perimeter with several downpipes to the tanks. About a quarter of the roof faces north, so most of the solar panels would go on a frame or a ground mount.

- **Unit 3.** About 3.6 metres of fall [TBV], and the lantern over the limit. A poor fit.

## Pros

- The least wall of any plan for the same floor, which makes it the cheapest shape to wall and to heat.
- Sheds the wind from any direction.
- A hearth at the centre warms both halves from one fire.
- Suits stone, earth and a pole roof built by the family.
- One roof and one kitchen, all on one level, with no height approval.

## Cons

- The second bedrooms face south-east and south-west and take little winter sun.
- Wedge-shaped rooms are harder to furnish than square ones.
- A reciprocal-frame roof is skilled work, and its timber needs protecting from fire with non-combustible sheeting and closed eaves.
- Little of the roof faces north for solar panels.
- A round house is unusual, and its design, colours and materials must be acceptable to all shareholders [MS].

## What is still unknown

- Whether the shareholders would accept a round house.
- How the reciprocal-frame roof would be engineered for the municipality's building plans.
- Whether a wood-burning hearth at the centre can be flued through the lantern.
- How strong the wind on the knoll is, which would show how much the round form gains.
- Whether the CMP would approve the patio, as landscaping and earthworks need its sign-off [MS].
