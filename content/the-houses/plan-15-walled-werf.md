> **These plans are not agreed.** The Haupt family drew them in October 2026 for discussion among themselves. Nobody outside the family has been asked: not the company, its Community Management Panel (CMP) or its Board, not the residents and not Hessequa Municipality. They will be put to the company through the CMP once the family has agreed what to propose.

## The goal

> Bring together what the fourteen plans have shown: a house that is simple to build, with every main room facing north, and the sheltered outdoor rooms of the courtyard and H-plans made by stone walls in place of building walls.

## What the other plans showed

- **One shared kitchen** answers the question of whether two households make two dwellings ([[the-houses/plan-9-shared-kitchen-and-patio|Plan 9]]).
- **One storey** keeps both halves step-free and needs no height approval ([[the-houses/plan-10-sunspace-shared-kitchen|Plan 10]]).
- **Courts** give outdoor rooms that count nothing toward the 200 m² ([[the-houses/plan-11-courtyard-house|Plans 11]] and [[the-houses/plan-12-h-plan|12]]). Building them as wings costs wall, valleys and east and west roofs.
- **A simple roof** with no valleys is the cheapest to build and the least likely to leak ([[the-houses/plan-13-barn|Plan 13]]).
- **Glass needs open sky** to its north in winter, so a pergola stands at least 4.5 metres back ([[the-houses/plan-10-sunspace-shared-kitchen|Plan 10]]).
- **A straight bar** has much less wall than a U or an H. Only the circle has less ([[the-houses/plan-14-roundhouse|Plan 14]]), and it costs the bedrooms their sun.

The house is one bar, 25 metres long and 8 metres deep, under one roof plane that rises from about 2.8 metres at the north eave to about 4 metres at the south, and faces north. There is no ridge and no valley. The whole roof, about 200 m², faces north for solar panels and drains to one gutter on the north side.

Half A takes the west 10.5 metres and Half B the east, each 84 m², with the shared core of 32 m² between them. In each half, both bedrooms and the living room stand along the north wall, so every main room takes the winter sun:

- **Two bedrooms** of about 16 m² at the outer end, and **a living room** of 17 m² beside the core.
- **A passage** along the south side, a step-free bathroom, a store and study, and the half's own entry and front door on the south.

The core holds the shared kitchenette and dining room of 18 m², facing north onto the court, with the laundry and the plant room behind it opening south onto the yard. The south wall can be site stone, heavy and solid, with small windows.

The courts are made by dry-stone walls 1.2 to 1.5 metres high, built from the rock cleared for the slab. Walls open to the sky are neither floor area nor area under roof:

- **The north court,** 25 by 10 metres, about 250 m², walled on the east and west and along most of the north, with a gap left open to the view. Its north wall also holds the terrace level.
- **The arrival yard,** 25 by 6 metres, about 150 m², walled on three sides with a gate to the track. Both front doors open onto it, the parking stands in it on open gravel, and the laundry opens onto it for the washing line.

The approval asks for the clearing and the homes to be screened from neighbours, the roads and the nature reserves within view [IC]. Stone walls and planting along them do part of that work. The ground is very rocky [MS], so the walls turn the rock from the platform into the courts.

- **The sun terrace,** about 112 m², runs the full length of the house, 4.5 metres deep and open, so the winter sun reaches all the north glass.
- **Beyond it, under an open pergola** of about 66 m², stand the outdoor kitchen of 21 m², the dining terrace of 30 m² and two herb beds, about 0.3 metres lower.
- **A fire circle** of about 36 m² and **a kitchen garden** of about 36 m² fill the corners against the walls, sheltered on two sides.

In all there are about 199 m² of paving and 51 m² of beds inside the walls, all outside the 200 m². Rain on the paving runs to the beds and the garden, and out through a gap in the north wall to a swale, as the approval asks for sustainable drainage [IC].

## How each household would use it

- **House 1.** Michael and his daughter in one half, a bedroom each. Veronica in the other, with her second bedroom as a room for visitors or for work, and the store and study for her own use.
- **House 2.** Robert and his wife in one half. Their mother and her partner in the other, with level parking in the yard, their front door a few steps away, and every room on one level.

The two households share the kitchenette and the court. Their living rooms stand either side of the kitchenette, and their bedrooms at the far ends of the house. Whether both want to share one kitchen is theirs to decide.

## On the three sites

The site figures come from [[the-houses/layout-d-site-development-plan|Layout D]], which modelled each platform from 10-metre contours. They are [TBV] until the platforms have been walked.

- **Unit 1.** The best fit. The ground falls about 0.6 metres across the house [TBV], and about 1.3 metres from the south wall to the court's north wall [TBV], which holds the terrace with about a metre of fill behind it.
- **Main Dwelling.** The ground falls about 1 metre across the house and about 2.1 metres to the court's north wall [TBV], which would become a retaining wall nearly 2 metres high. A fair fit. Part B, the shared building, could stand along the yard's south side under its own roof, or extend the bar at one end under the same roof plane.
- **Unit 3.** The ground falls about 4 metres across the house and court [TBV]. A poor fit.

## Pros

- Every main room in both halves faces north: both bedrooms and the living room.
- The simplest roof of all the plans: one plane, no ridge, no valleys, all of it facing north for solar panels.
- Two outdoor rooms of 250 m² and 150 m², sheltered by walls built from the site's own rock, none of it counting toward the 200 m².
- One storey, one kitchen, every room step-free, and no height approval: the house stands about 4 metres at its highest.
- Level parking at the front doors, out of sight of the court.
- The walls help screen the house, as the approval asks [IC].

## Cons

- The living rooms are 17 m², smaller than in the courtyard house and the H-plan, though the court is far larger.
- Dry-stone walls take a great deal of hand work, even with the stone on site.
- A long bar puts the two households' bedrooms 25 metres apart and their living rooms side by side, with the kitchenette between them for sound.
- The walls are a landscaping and earthworks job that needs the CMP's sign-off [MS].
- Leaves and embers gather against walls. The foot of each wall needs keeping clear in the fire season.

## What is still unknown

- Whether the CMP and the shareholders would approve the walls and the courts, as landscaping and earthworks need the CMP's sign-off [MS] and the design of every home must be acceptable to all shareholders [MS].
- How much stone the platform will give, which depends on how deep the rock lies.
- Whether walls of this height need the municipality's approval.
- Whether the walls and courts count toward the disturbed footprint named in the approval.
- Whether the municipality would treat a wall that holds more than a metre of fill as a retaining wall needing an engineer's design.
